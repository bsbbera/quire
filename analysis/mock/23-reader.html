The Lamp Room, chapter 9

Eleven weeks is a long time for a light to keep itself, and the log said nothing about it. Cormack read the last page twice on the boat over, holding it out of the spray, and found only the ordinary entries a keeper makes when there is nothing to make an entry about: wind, visibility, the hour the lamp was lit, the hour it was put out. The hand did not change. The hand did not tire. On the eleventh week it simply stopped, mid-column, as though the man writing it had been called away and meant to come back.

The tender put him off at the landing and did not wait. He watched it turn in the sound with the particular haste of a crew that has somewhere else to be, and then he carried his box up the hundred and forty steps cut into the rock, and let himself in through a door that was not locked.

Inside, everything was in its place. That was the first thing, and it took him some time to understand why it troubled him. The kitchen was swept. The brass was bright. There were two cups on the shelf above the range and both of them were clean and dry, set mouth-down the way a careful man leaves them. The oil store was full to within an inch of where the record said it should be.

He counted it again in the morning, and again on the second evening, and the figure did not move. Somebody had been burning oil at exactly the rate the book required, and writing none of it down, for seventy-seven days.

On the third night the wind came round to the north and he went up to the lamp room at half past four to find the wick already trimmed. He stood on the last step with the lamp turning above him and his hand on the rail, and he did not go in for a while.

In the morning he wrote in the log for the first time. He put the date, and the wind, and the visibility, and the hour the lamp was lit. Then he sat with the pen over the column for the keeper’s name and, after a time, he wrote the name that was already there.
